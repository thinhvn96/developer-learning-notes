Queue-based load leveling

System Design · Lesson 0007 · step 6 of 11 · Core · ~10 minutes · Home · System Design ladder · Roadmap

Today's win: explain how a queue levels load between a producer and a service, and its limits.

Idea

Place a queue between the task and the service: "The queue acts as a buffer and stores the message until the service retrieves it." Tasks and service run asynchronously; the service handles messages "at its own pace even when concurrent tasks generate a high volume of requests". Benefits: availability (service delays do not immediately affect the app), scalability, cost control (provision for average, not peak).[1]

java system-design/examples/LoadLevelingDemo.java
burst of 100 accepted in 0 ms; queue length now ~98
all processed after 5015 ms; max concurrent work at the service = 2

The burst was accepted instantly; the two workers (100 ms per task) drained it at their own pace and the service never saw more than 2 concurrent tasks. Source: LoadLevelingDemo.java.

Considerations

  • If the average producer rate exceeds the consumer rate, the queue grows and latency increases: monitor depth and scale within safe limits or shed work.
  • Queues are one-way; a caller expecting a reply needs another mechanism.
  • Durability matters: a broker that does not persist can lose enqueued work.
  • At-least-once delivery means idempotent consumers; route poison messages to a dead-letter queue.
  • Ordering is not preserved under parallel consumers.[1]

Not for low-latency synchronous responses.[1] Implementations in this course: RabbitMQ and Kafka.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory. One attempt each.

1. What does the queue buffer between?

It sits between a task and the service it invokes.[1]

2. Producers faster than consumers on average. Result?

The queue continues to grow and latency increases.[1]

3. Poor fit when...?

It is not suitable when the caller needs a low-latency synchronous response.[1]

4. Cost benefit?

You need enough instances for average rather than peak load.[1]
